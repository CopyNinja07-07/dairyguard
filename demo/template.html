The Science

Why it works, and how we know.

The biological clock

Milk at 4, 8 and 12 °C crosses the limit at very different times. Switch to the bacteria's own clock, which runs faster when it's warm, and the three curves become one. That's why one running total of the log is all a monitor needs.

The heat test simulated

Growing bacteria give off a tiny amount of heat. Hold a spoonful of milk at 20 °C in a calorimeter and time when the heat shows up. Busy bacteria show up sooner: one number for what the thermometer can't see.

Saying "how likely" simulated

When a forecast says 70%, is it right 70% of the time? Average miss; smaller is better. In the standard world the comparison includes a model trained on 4,000 labelled trips, tested on the same 4,000 new trips; DairyGuard needed no labels.

Checked against a real experiment real data

Milk stored at 4 °C, with and without two 4-hour warm spells at 29 °C (Lin et al., 2016). The model never saw these times.

Predicting studies it never saw real data

Five temperature laws, including a neural network, fitted on all Listeria studies in ComBase but one and tested on the one left out. Accuracy factor: 1.0 is perfect.

Next in our lab

DairyGuard Batch: learning each batch

The evidence above says the limit is the batch, not the thermometer: real growth rates differ between studies by about 25%, and a flexible model learns nothing new from growth rates alone. Our next model keeps DairyGuard Kinetic's core and learns each batch's biology from complete growth curves and simple tests of the milk at loading. It will appear here, next to DairyGuard Kinetic, once it has been built and tested on real data.
