\noindent{\small\textbf{Abstract}\enspace A cold-chain logger records temperature, but bacterial growth in milk also depends on strain, starting load and lag, which a thermometer cannot see. We quantify how much of the error in predicting spoilage from a temperature log arises from this unobserved biology, from the sensor, from the sampling interval and from the decision rule. Treating growth as a non-isothermal kinetic process, we prove that under the square-root (Ratkowsky) rate law the Baranyi--Roberts model, a Gompertz shelf-life accumulator and the calorimetric heat flow of a growing culture all depend on the thermal history only through a generalized time, analogous to Ozawa's. The rate law implies an apparent activation energy falling from 109 to 66\,kJ\,mol$^{-1}$ between 4 and 13\degC. Error propagation predicts that biology outweighs the error of a $\pm0.5$\degC{} sensor about sevenfold, and a Monte Carlo benchmark of 240\,000 simulated trips, parametrised from published and ComBase data, confirms it. Unobserved biology costs \NumBaseGapMin--\NumBaseGapMax{} in the area under the ROC curve (AUC); a platinum resistance sensor gains at most \NumBaseSensMax; hourly sampling matches minute sampling in most settings; and no Arrhenius or $Q_{10}$ integrator outperforms the square-root integrator in any of 60 settings. An isothermal calorimetric detection time of the lot, combined with the log, recovers \CalFracMin--\CalFracMax\,\% of the biological loss. The model reproduces a fluctuating-temperature experiment within \NumLinErrAbs\,\% and predicts held-out \textit{Listeria monocytogenes} growth rates with an accuracy factor of \NumLisAccHeld, and a Monte Carlo probability is far better calibrated than a single alarm.\par}
