Why we started

It began as a box. It became a question.

For the Smart India Hackathon our team designed a cold-chain pod for dairy logistics: an ESP32, a DS18B20 temperature probe and a Peltier cooler, with an app that warns when milk has been too warm for too long. Building its shelf-life model raised a question the hardware could not answer.

01 · THE POD

A logger watches

The probe records the temperature of the pack every few minutes, faithfully and cheaply.

02 · THE GAP

Bacteria hide

How many bacteria the milk started with, which kind, how fast they wake up: no thermometer can see any of it.

03 · THE QUESTION

How much can a log tell?

When a monitor is wrong, is it the cheap sensor, the reading interval, the formula, or the hidden biology? We set out to measure each.

How we did it

From records to results.

STEP 01

Collected the data

Searched ComBase, the public database of bacterial growth experiments, for milk. Transcribed organism, temperature, pH, water activity, source study and growth rate for each record, and converted every rate from log₁₀ to natural-log units. Added a published fluctuating-temperature experiment (Lin et al. 2016) and the FSSAI limit.

373 records52 sourcesevery value sourced or flagged
STEP 02

Fitted and trained the models

Fitted five temperature laws by least squares on log growth rates, including a monotone neural network trained as an ensemble of five. Judged every law on data it had not seen: each Listeria study left out in turn.

5 laws20 studies held outneural ensemble × 5
STEP 03

Built a world where the truth is known

Simulated deliveries minute by minute: fridges, warm spells, packaging, three thermometers, four reading intervals, and hidden biology drawn for every trip. The reference model decides the truth; the monitor sees only the log.

240,000 trips5 worlds × 12 settingsfixed seeds
STEP 04

Tested it hard

Scored every rule by AUC, compared rules on identical trips with a paired bootstrap, checked calibration, pitted the monitor against a model trained on 4,000 labelled trips, and reran everything to confirm identical results.

27 automated testsexact rerun
STEP 05

Proved why it works

Showed that bacterial growth depends on a temperature history only through one running total, a biological clock, the same idea as Ozawa's generalized time in thermal analysis. It predicts every number in the loss budget.

exact proof
STEP 06

Shipped it

Packaged the monitor as a Python library and command-line tool, wrote it again in C for microcontrollers, and built the browser app. Wrote it all up in a book and an architecture white paper.

PythonCJavaScript
What we found

The thermometer isn't the problem. The hidden biology is.

We scored every way of reading a log on one question: can it tell the cartons that crossed the bacterial limit from those that didn't? A coin flip scores 50, a perfect method 100.

Lost because no one can see the bacteria simulation27–40 pts
Spread of growth rates between real studies real data25%
Won back by one heat test of the milk simulation69–94% of the gap
Gained by a lab-grade thermometer simulation≤ 2 pts
Lost by reading hourly, not every minute simulationabout 0

Real data. On studies they never saw, five laws including a neural network score within a narrow band; growth rates differ between studies by about 25%.

The monitor under test. As well calibrated as a model trained on 4,000 labelled trips, stable from 80 plausible batches, and robust to correlated biology.

"Crossing the limit" means the bacteria count passed the FSSAI hygiene limit for pasteurised milk. It is a count, not a taste test or a safety verdict. The heat test is a calorimetry measurement, simulated so far.

The key idea

Bacteria keep their own clock.

Milk at 4, 8 and 12 °C crosses the limit at very different times. Switch to the bacteria's own clock, which runs faster when it's warm, and the three curves become one.

That one running total is all a monitor needs from the log, which is why a cheap thermometer and hourly readings are enough, and why the biology is what's left.

Where it stops

The evidence points to the batch.

25%

Real growth rates differ between studies by a quarter. The biology a log can't see is real, and large.

No gain

A flexible neural law learns nothing new from growth rates alone. Better formulas aren't the answer.

±16 h

The monitor's estimate of hours left is cautious but imprecise, because the batch is unknown.
