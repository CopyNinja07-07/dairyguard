A research project on safe food

Every glass of milk has a journey.

From the farm to your fridge, milk is handled by many hands and carried through many temperatures. We set out to learn one simple thing: what decides whether it stays safe?

Our belief

Safe food should not depend on luck. People who care about their health deserve to know that the milk in the glass was looked after the whole way.

Unsafe food makes about 600 million people ill every year, and around 420,000 die, according to the World Health Organization. A large share of those deaths are in South-East Asia, including India. Cold, reliable handling is one of the simplest protections we have.

Our project looks at one small piece of that chain: the temperature record of a milk delivery, and how well it can tell us whether the milk went off. We study spoilage, and one dangerous germ, Listeria. We do not measure sickness itself.

The big one

The thing that matters most is the one thing no thermometer can see.

We tested this on thousands of computer-simulated milk deliveries, where we knew the true answer. We scored each method on one question: given a delivery's temperature record, can you tell the cartons that will spoil from the ones that will not? A coin flip scores 50. A perfect method scores 100.

What it means

Know the start, then keep it cold.

Money is better spent on knowing how clean the milk was at the start than on fancier sensors.
A smarter score beats a plain "was it above 5 °C" alarm, but only by a small amount.
Systems should say "there is a 70% chance this batch is at risk", not just flash red or green.
At home, the simple habits still work: keep milk cold, put it back quickly, and mind the date.
Being honest

What we have not shown.

Our trucks and fridges are simulated, so real-world logs are the next test. The growth numbers come from published studies and a public database, and some of that data was copied by hand from screen recordings. Products that monitor milk already exist, and we are still checking exactly what is new here. This is research in progress, not medical or food-safety advice.
