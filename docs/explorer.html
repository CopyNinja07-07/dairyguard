How much of milk spoilage can a temperature record predict? Run a trip through the shelf-life calculation, then browse the benchmark that tests it against a detailed reference model.

1. Biology is the limit. What a thermometer cannot see (strain, starting bacteria, lag) costs far more accuracy than any sensor or sampling choice.
2. Simple rules come close. A degree-hours count gets nearly the same accuracy as the full growth model.
3. Say the uncertainty. A probability is better calibrated than a single yes/no alarm.
Partly assumed. Growth rate and minimum temperature come from published studies and from public ComBase records; strain spread, starting bacteria and some lags are assumptions, and no new laboratory counts exist. This models bacterial growth (spoilage bacteria, and Listeria as a case study), not illness, and it is not a safety verdict.

Does the model match real experiments?

The simulator's kernel was fitted to growth rates published by Lin and colleagues (2016) for Pseudomonas fluorescens in milk, then asked to predict something it was not fitted to: the shelf life after two short warm periods.

Only the constant 4 °C result was close by construction. The excursion result is the real test. It is one experiment with inoculated UHT milk, so it supports the model's behaviour and does not validate it.

Real records from ComBase

Natural spoilage bacteria. 51 growth-rate records of aerobic spoilage bacteria in pasteurised milk (2.7 to 14.9 °C) show the same temperature shape as the model, but real flora grow about 1.6 times slower than the lab-isolate parameters predict. Choose "Fitted to real milk flora" in the selector above: the conclusions do not change.

The records were transcribed by optical character recognition from screen recordings of the database and have not been checked against it. Treat the fits as provisional.

Why it matters

WHO estimated in 2015 that foodborne disease causes about 600 million illnesses and 420,000 deaths a year, and 175,000 deaths a year in the South-East Asia region. Those deaths come mostly from agents other than the bacteria modelled here, and the estimates are not specific to milk. Spoilage bacteria waste food and money. Listeria grows even at refrigerator temperatures, and the data show it speeds up steeply when milk warms. Warm handling affects both. This page estimates growth, not illness risk.

What this does not show

  • Strain spread and starting load are assumed, and no new laboratory counts exist. Results are patterns within the simulation, not findings about real milk.
  • The estimator and the reference model share structure, so the kinetic score is expected to beat simple thresholds slightly. The benchmark measures how much accuracy sensors, sampling and unobservable biology take away.
  • About half the trips are spoiled by construction, so predictive values are not field rates.
  • No new plate counts have been measured. Real counts, sensor calibration and field logging are the next steps (see the repository roadmap).
  • ComBase records were transcribed automatically and are unverified; the Listeria held-out test is split by condition, not by study.
